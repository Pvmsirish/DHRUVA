The roastery

A house in Assagao that smells permanently of caramel.

Latitude 15° started as a stubborn argument. In 2016 two of us were working in a café in Panjim where the house blend was bought by the sack, roasted somewhere unknown, and arrived tasting of ash and burnt sugar no matter how carefully we brewed it. We knew the estates were only a night's drive east. We knew the fruit coming off Baba Budangiri and the Biligiri hills was extraordinary. Somewhere between the hill and the cup, all of that was being incinerated.

So we bought a sample roaster, rented the back room of a crumbling Portuguese house off the Assagao–Anjuna road, and spent eleven months getting it wrong in public. Our first customers were surfers, chefs and a very patient bakery in Siolim who kept buying coffee we would not sell today. By 2019 we had the drum roaster, three estate relationships, and a rule we have never broken: no bag leaves the roastery more than nine days after it comes off the cooling tray.

Everything we do is downstream of that argument. We roast light-to-medium because the sugars in high-grown Indian arabica deserve to survive. We publish what we pay per kilogram of parchment, because "direct trade" is a phrase that means nothing without a number attached. And we cup every batch before it ships — sixteen bowls on a Wednesday morning, the whole team, no exceptions, including the two people whose job is mostly packing tape.

Cherry to cup

Four things happen between the hill and your kitchen.

There is no mystery to good coffee, only a long chain of small decisions where somebody could have cut a corner and didn't. Here is ours, in order.

  1. 01

    We go up in December

    Harvest runs November to February. We drive up, sleep on the estate, taste the day's picking and agree a price per kilo of parchment before a single sack moves.

  2. 02

    Milled and rested

    Parchment rests six to eight weeks at the estate, then goes to a mill in Hassan for hulling and screen sorting. We take screen 16 and above, hand-picked twice.

  3. 03

    Profiled by hand

    Every lot gets four to nine sample roasts before we commit a profile. We log charge temperature, turnaround, first crack and development time for each batch.

  4. 04

    Packed the same week

    Bags are filled by hand on Wednesday and Saturday, dated in ink, and handed to the courier before the coffee has finished degassing properly.

Brew guides

Four recipes we actually use every morning.

These are starting points, not scripture. If the cup tastes thin and sour, grind finer or use hotter water. If it tastes dry and bitter, grind coarser or pour less. Everything else is detail.

V60 · the clean one

The best way to taste what an estate actually did. We use it for every light roast in the range, and it is the method our cupping table most closely resembles. A 02 cone, a paper filter rinsed with boiling water, and a scale you can read.

Dose
15 g
Water
250 g
Temp
94 °C
Time
2:45
  1. Rinse the paper thoroughly, discard the water, and add coffee ground to the texture of table salt.
  2. Bloom with 45 g of water. Swirl the cone gently, don't stir. Wait 40 seconds — you should see the bed dome and crack.
  3. Pour to 150 g in slow concentric circles, staying off the paper wall.
  4. Pour to 250 g once the bed has dropped about halfway. Give the cone one final swirl to flatten the bed.
  5. Drawdown should finish around 2:45. Longer means grind coarser; much shorter means grind finer.

AeroPress · the forgiving one

What we pack when we travel and what we hand to anyone who says brewing is too fiddly. Inverted, one paper filter, and a two-minute steep. It flatters medium roasts and rescues coffee ground slightly wrong.

Dose
17 g
Water
220 g
Temp
88 °C
Time
2:15
  1. Assemble inverted with the plunger pushed in one centimetre. Grind medium-fine, like coarse sand.
  2. Add all the coffee, then all 220 g of water in one confident pour. Stir three times, north to south.
  3. Cap with a rinsed filter and steep for two minutes flat.
  4. Flip onto a sturdy mug and press slowly — aim for thirty seconds of push. Stop at the hiss.
  5. Top with 40 g hot water if you want a longer cup. Do not press the hiss; that's where the bitterness lives.

South Indian filter · the one we grew up on

A stainless steel dabara set, a fine grind, and patience. This is the method half of Goa's kitchens already own, and our Susegad blend was built for it. Chicory optional, and we won't judge you either way — though we would try it clean at least once.

Dose
22 g
Water
180 g
Temp
96 °C
Time
15 min
  1. Grind fine — finer than filter, coarser than espresso. The powder should clump when you squeeze it.
  2. Load the upper chamber, level it, and press gently with the plunger disc. Do not compact it like a tamp.
  3. Pour water just off the boil in one go, cover, and leave it completely alone for twelve to fifteen minutes.
  4. You should collect roughly 90 ml of thick decoction. Anything faster means your grind is too coarse.
  5. Mix one part decoction to three parts hot milk, sweeten to taste, and pull it between tumbler and dabara until it foams.

Cold brew · the one for April in Goa

Between March and June, hot coffee stops being a good idea by nine in the morning. This makes a concentrate that keeps ten days in the fridge and dilutes into everything from a tall glass with tonic to an emergency evening iced latte.

Dose
100 g
Water
700 g
Temp
Room
Time
16 hrs
  1. Grind coarse, like raw demerara sugar. Naturals and honeys shine here; save the delicate washed lots for filter.
  2. Combine grounds and room-temperature water in a jar. Stir until every particle is wet, then seal.
  3. Leave on the counter for four hours, then in the fridge for another twelve. Do not go past twenty hours.
  4. Strain through a fine mesh, then once more through a rinsed paper filter for a cleaner glass.
  5. Dilute one part concentrate to two parts water, milk or soda. Serve over a lot of ice, always.

The tasting room

Come and drink it where it's roasted.

The front two rooms of the Assagao house are open to anyone who wanders in. There is a four-seat bar facing the roaster, a courtyard under a jackfruit tree with eleven mismatched chairs, and a filter batch on the go from opening until we run out. No laptops after eleven on weekends — that's the only rule, and it's non-negotiable in season.

Every Saturday at nine we cup whatever came off the drum that week. It's free, it's loud, and it lasts about ninety minutes. You do not need to know anything about coffee; you need to be able to slurp from a spoon without being self-conscious about it. Questions

Things people ask us, honestly answered.

We roast on Tuesdays and Fridays and dispatch within forty-eight hours of the batch coming off the cooling tray. Depending on where you are, that means the bag lands between three and seven days post-roast. Every bag is stamped with the actual roast date, not a "best before" that hides it.

Coffee is not at its best on day one, though. Light roasts need a week to degas properly. If you open a bag on day three and it tastes flat and gassy, close it and wait four more days — it will be a different coffee.

Whole bean, if you have any kind of grinder at all — even a modest hand grinder will beat pre-ground coffee that has been sitting in a bag losing aromatics for a fortnight. Ground coffee goes stale in roughly fifteen minutes; whole beans take weeks.

That said, we would rather you drink our coffee ground than not drink it. Tell us your brewer at checkout and we will grind it correctly on our EK, free, and pack it in a valve bag to slow things down as much as physics allows.

We ship across India. Metros usually take two days, everywhere else three to four, and the Northeast and Andamans occasionally five. Shipping is ₹90 flat and free above ₹1,500 or on any subscription. Within Goa we deliver ourselves twice a week and it is always free.

We do not ship internationally yet. Customs paperwork for roasted coffee is genuinely miserable and we would rather spend that time roasting. If you are visiting, order ahead and collect from the roastery.
